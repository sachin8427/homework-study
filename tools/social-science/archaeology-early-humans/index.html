Archaeology & Early Humans

How historians know the past, how archaeologists dig it up, and how the Neolithic Revolution changed everything.

How Historians Study the Past sources

Primary sources were created during the time being studied — letters, diaries, photographs, artifacts. Secondary sources are created after the event, and interpret, combine, or summarize primary sources to help explain the past. Historians often disagree on how to interpret the same evidence — that disagreement is normal and is called historiography: the study of how historians study, interpret, and write about history.

Famous find · Pompeii & Herculaneum Buried by the eruption of Mt. Vesuvius in A.D. 79 — preserved evidence of how everyday Romans lived.
Famous find · The Rosetta Stone Found in Rosetta, Egypt in 1799 by Napoleon's troops. Inscribed in three scripts — Greek, Demotic, and Hieroglyphics. Jean-François Champollion deciphered it by comparing the three, unlocking the ability to read Egyptian hieroglyphics.
Famous find · Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania Louis and Mary Leakey led a dig that found human footprints 3.7 million years old — nicknamed the "Cradle of Mankind," helping prove human evolution began in Africa.

The Ages of Early Humans sequence

Named for the main material used for tools, in order: Paleolithic (Old Stone Age) → Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age) → Neolithic (New Stone Age) → Bronze Age (bronze = copper + tin) → Iron Age (still going on today).

The Neolithic Revolution cause & effect

This is the single biggest chain of cause-and-effect in early human history — follow it step by step:

Case study · Çatalhöyük (southern Turkey) A 9,000-year-old, extremely well-preserved Neolithic settlement. Homes were built back-to-back and entered through the roof. Residents buried their dead under the floors of their houses and decorated walls with art — evidence they valued community and spirituality.
Case study · Tell Abu Hureyra (Euphrates River, Syria) Occupied roughly 11,500–7,000 B.C. Residents first hunted gazelle and other game, then around 9,700 B.C. began deliberately planting wild grains — one of the earliest known transitions from hunting to farming.
