Field Dispatch · Grade 5

United States, 1763 – 1900s

From taxes and tea to railroads and cities — how a set of British colonies became an industrial nation. Follow the causes, not just the dates.

The chain of events

Every arrow means "which caused." Read down each chain like a story, not a list of dates to memorize.

1
The American Revolution
1754 – 1783
1754
French and Indian War begins
Britain and France go to war over control of North American land and the fur trade; American colonists fight alongside British troops.
Britain wins, but at a cost ↓
1763
War ends — Britain is deep in debt
Britain now controls huge new territory but is broke from the war. Under mercantilism — the idea that colonies exist to enrich the mother country — Britain decides the colonies should help pay the bill.
Britain starts taxing directly ↓
1765
Britain passes the Stamp Act
The first direct tax on the colonies, on paper goods — but colonists have no vote in Parliament to object.
because of this ↓
1770
Boston Massacre
Anger over taxes and British troops in Boston boils over into a deadly clash in the streets.
which fed into ↓
1773
Boston Tea Party
Colonists dump British tea into the harbor to protest "no taxation without representation."
Britain punishes them ↓
1774
Intolerable Acts
Britain closes Boston's port and cracks down — colonies respond by forming the Continental Congress.
tension explodes into war ↓
1775
Lexington & Concord
The first shots of the war are fired when British troops try to seize colonial weapons.
the colonies declare why ↓
1776
Declaration of Independence
Written mainly by Thomas Jefferson, it lays out the colonies' reasons for breaking away from Britain.
war continues, France joins the colonists ↓
1781
British surrender at Yorktown
With French help, Washington's army traps the British army — effectively ending the fighting.
peace is made official ↓
1783
Treaty of Paris
Britain formally recognizes the United States as an independent nation.
2
Building a Government
1781 – 1791
1781
Articles of Confederation take effect
The first American government — deliberately weak, with no power to tax or enforce laws, because states feared another king.
too weak to work ↓
1787
Constitutional Convention
Leaders meet in Philadelphia and write a new Constitution creating three branches of government that check each other's power.
states demand a promise ↓
1791
Bill of Rights ratified
The first 10 amendments guarantee individual freedoms — the price of getting states to approve the Constitution.
3
Westward Expansion
1803 – 1849
1803
Louisiana Purchase
The U.S. buys land from France, instantly doubling the size of the country.
settlers push into Native land ↓
1830
Indian Removal Act
The government forces Native nations off their homelands; thousands die on the Trail of Tears.
belief in "Manifest Destiny" grows ↓
1846
U.S.–Mexican War begins
Disputes over Texas and the idea that the U.S. was destined to stretch coast to coast lead to war with Mexico.
U.S. wins new territory ↓
1849
California Gold Rush
Gold is discovered on land just won from Mexico, drawing waves of settlers west almost overnight.
4
The Civil War Era
1850 – 1865
1850s
North and South grow apart
The North industrializes with factories and free labor; the South's economy depends on enslaved labor and cash crops like cotton.
disputes over new territories ↓
1850s
Compromises over slavery fail to hold
Deals like the Compromise of 1850 try to balance free and slave states, but the arguments keep resurfacing.
a president is elected ↓
1860
Lincoln elected president
Southern states fear he'll end slavery, even though he hadn't yet proposed to at that point.
states secede ↓
1861
Fort Sumter is attacked
Confederate forces fire on a Union fort in South Carolina — the war begins.
the war's purpose sharpens ↓
1863
Emancipation Proclamation
Lincoln declares enslaved people in Confederate states free, reframing the war around ending slavery.
the Union wins ↓
1865
War ends; 13th Amendment ratified
The Union wins, and the 13th Amendment abolishes slavery across the entire United States.
5
Industrialization & Immigration
1865 – 1900s
1869
Transcontinental Railroad completed
Rail lines connect the whole country, making it fast and cheap to move goods and people.
factories multiply ↓
1870s–90s
Inventions accelerate industry
The telephone, electric light, and new machinery let factories run faster and cities grow taller.
factories need workers ↓
1892
Ellis Island opens
Millions of immigrants arrive seeking factory jobs and a new life, becoming the workforce of American industry.
cities can't keep up ↓
1900s
Rapid urbanization
Cities grow faster than housing and sanitation can handle, crowding immigrant and worker families into tenements — setting the stage for reform movements.
Memory tricks

Real mnemonic devices for the parts of this era that are hardest to keep straight.

The setup, before Sam even shows up
War → Debt → Mercantilism → Tax
Four words, one domino chain: the French and Indian War (1754–63) leaves Britain in Debt. Mercantilism — the belief that colonies exist to make money for the home country — means Britain expects the colonies to help pay it off. That's why the very next thing that happens is a Tax (the Stamp Act, 1765). Every "why did Britain tax the colonies?" question traces back to these four words.
Order of events · road to revolution
"Sam Brews Tea In Lexington"
Stamp Act (1765) → Boston Massacre (1770) → Tea Party (1773) → Intolerable Acts (1774) → Lexington & Concord (1775). Bonus: it's even named after Samuel Adams, a real Son of Liberty who helped organize the Tea Party. (This picks up right where "War → Debt → Mercantilism → Tax" leaves off.)
3 branches of government
Legislative · Executive · Judicial
Each branch's job starts with the same sound as its name: Legislative makes Laws, Executive Enforces them, Judicial Judges them. You don't need a separate rhyme — the name IS the clue.
Order of events · westward push
"Lewis Ran Manifest Miles Gladly"
Louisiana Purchase (1803) → Removal Act (1830) → Manifest Destiny belief spreads (1840s) → Mexican War (1846) → Gold Rush (1849). "Lewis" nods to Lewis & Clark, who explored the Louisiana Purchase first.
Dates worth anchoring
1776 · 1787 · 1861–1865
1776 = Declaration of Independence (tie it to July 4th fireworks you already know). 1787 = the Constitution is written (think: "17-87, we wrote the laws" — four years after the war officially ended in 1783). 1861–1865 = Civil War (exactly the years between Lincoln's election and his second term).
The number that matters
13th Amendment = ends slavery
Trick: the original 13 colonies started the country, and the 13th Amendment finished the job of making it a country where slavery was illegal everywhere. Same number, bookends of the story.
