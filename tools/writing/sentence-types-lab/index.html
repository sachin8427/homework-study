Sentence Types Lab

Four formulas, two magic words, and a game to lock them into memory — built around your Amelia & Babe paragraph.

Every argument paragraph is really 3 moves, repeated twice: Claim → Evidence → Reasoning (the same CER pattern from your science labs — it works for writing too).

Claim the argument

WHO + SHOULD BE HONORED + STRONGEST REASON

Your topic sentence. Name the person and give the single biggest reason she deserves recognition — this is the promise the rest of the paragraph has to keep.

"Amelia Earhart deserves to be remembered because she proved women could achieve things the world said only men could do."

Evidence & Quotes the proof

SIGNAL PHRASE + "EXACT QUOTE" + (CITATION)

Three parts, every time:

1. Signal phrase — tell the reader a quote is coming: "According to the text," "The author explains," "In the passage, it says…"
2. Exact quote — copy the words precisely, inside quotation marks. Keep it short (under 15 words is plenty).
3. Citation — show where it came from, the way your teacher wants it formatted (page number, author, etc.).

Model (general example, not from your book): According to the article, cheetahs can "reach speeds of over sixty miles per hour in just a few seconds" (Smith, p. 12).

Your job: find the exact matching quote inside your own Amelia to Zora pages and drop it into this same 3-part formula.

Reasoning the "so what"

"THIS SHOWS / PROVES / DEMONSTRATES THAT…" + LINK BACK TO CLAIM

Don't just drop the quote and move on — explain why it matters. Pull a specific detail or number out of the quote and connect it directly back to your claim.

"This shows that Amelia didn't just fly — she flew farther and more dangerously than anyone had attempted before, proving her courage matched her skill."

🔑 The Paragraph Formula

Stack these 5 moves in order and you've hit Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning on the rubric:

1. Claim 2. Evidence #1 (quote) 3. Reasoning #1 4. Evidence #2 (quote) 5. Reasoning #2
Weave in the sentence-level stuff: your dress-ups and openers can go anywhere in these 5 moves — a strong opener works great on the claim, and a who/which clause dress-up fits naturally right after a quote. Your compound and complex sentences (next tab) work especially well inside your Reasoning sentences.

Simple 1 clause

IC

One independent clause — one subject + one verb that makes a complete thought, standing all by itself. No helper words needed.

"Babe played golf."

Compound 2 clauses

IC , FANBOYS IC

Two independent clauses, joined by a comma + a FANBOYS word. Both halves could stand alone as their own sentence.

"Babe won three gold medals, but judges denied her a fourth."

🔑 FANBOYS — the compound connector word

For And Nor But Or Yet So

Complex 1 main + 1 support

AAAWWUBBIS-word, IC  ·  IC AAAWWUBBIS-word

One dependent clause (starts with an AAAWWUBBIS word and can't stand alone) + one independent clause. If the dependent clause comes first, put a comma after it.

"After she left the Olympics, Babe became a professional golfer."
"Amelia went flying through the air because the cab hit a rock."

🔑 AAAWWUBBIS — the complex connector word

After Although As When While Until Because Before If Since

Compound-Complex 3+ clauses

AAAWWUBBIS-word IC , IC FANBOYS IC

Mix both formulas: at least 2 independent clauses (joined with a FANBOYS word) + 1 dependent clause (starting with an AAAWWUBBIS word).

"When Amelia turned twenty-four, she bought her own plane, and she quit her old job."
Quick test: Cover up everything before the comma. If what's left could be its own sentence, and what's covered could ALSO be its own sentence → it's compound. If what's covered trails off and needs the rest → it's complex.
For your paragraph: the rubric wants at least 1 compound sentence and 1 complex sentence. Fastest path — open with an AAAWWUBBIS word for your complex sentence, and use "and" or "but" to link two related facts for your compound sentence.
