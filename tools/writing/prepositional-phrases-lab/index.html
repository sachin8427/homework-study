Prepositional Phrases Lab

Find the object of a preposition, cross out the phrase, and underline the real subject and verb — built around the one mix-up worth drilling: don't delete the verb by accident.

Definition
Object of a preposition
A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition and ends with a noun or pronoun. That final word is called the object of the preposition.
• Leah works with Dan. — Dan is a noun.
• Leah works with him. — him is a pronoun.

Method
Delete to find the subject and verb
Crossing out every prepositional phrase strips a sentence down to just the subject (underline once) and the verb (underline twice) — whatever is left over.
A branch of a tree fell during the blizzard.
Marla sends stories to me.

Watch out
Don't cross out the verb with the phrase
When a linking verb (is, are, was, were) sits right in front of a prepositional phrase, it's easy to drag it into the strike-through by mistake. The verb always stays — only the phrase after it gets crossed out.
The man in the black tie is from Oklahoma.
Check yourself: read what's left after crossing out. If there's no verb left standing, you crossed out too much.

Trick case
"But" can mean "except"
Most of the time but joins two ideas together. When it means except, it's working as a preposition instead, and everything after it is part of the phrase.
Everyone but Jessi looked for the missing sneaker.

Don't forget
Circle the whole phrase, not just the preposition
When the directions say "circle the prepositional phrase," the circle has to wrap the preposition and its object — not just the first word.
• among some people — not just among
• on an old couch — not just on
